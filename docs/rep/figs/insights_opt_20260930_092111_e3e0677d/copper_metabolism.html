Copper Metabolism 1995–2024

Six readings: five of the reference solver run, on how large the system is and how it grew, the whole chain in one picture, how much of the refinery feed is scrap, where each step has concentrated, and how trade links mines to smelters and cathode to its users; and a sixth, from IWCC data outside the model, on what the world's copper ends up in.

1 · System size

The system moves 34 Mt of copper a year across its boundary, 2.4 times what it did in 1995.

Ore in situ carries 28.5 Mt Cu into the chain and scrap another 5.4 Mt. Of that, 26.6 Mt leaves as cathode to final use, 5.4 Mt as tailings and 1.3 Mt as slag; the small remainder is booked as stock change. Every stage of the chain more than doubled since 1995, and the leach route (SX‑EW) grew 4.3×.

  • Growth is uniform through the pyrometallurgical core. Matte, anode and electrorefined copper all sit at ×2.24, concentrate at ×2.16: what a mass-balanced chain should show.
  • Cathode grew faster than the core (×2.46) because two routes added to it: SX‑EW cathode rose from 1.2 to 5.0 Mt Cu (×4.32) and scrap feed from 2.1 to 5.4 Mt (×2.51).
  • Mined copper outgrew concentrate (×2.33 vs ×2.16) because a growing share of ore is leached rather than milled; SX‑EW tailings rose ×4.4 with it.
  • Losses did not scale with output. Tailings ×2.01 and slag ×2.12 against final use ×2.52: the loss share of the boundary input fell from 23 % to 20 %.

2 · The chain in one picture

28 Mt in from the ground, 27 Mt of cathode out, and the traded part of every commodity made visible.

Hatched links cross a border. In 2024, 61 % of the world's concentrate is smelted in a region other than the one that mined it (11.0 of 18.1 Mt Cu), a third of the cathode is used abroad (9.1 of 27.2 Mt), 7 % of anode changes hands and matte barely moves at all.

  • Two routes out of the mine. Of the 28.5 Mt Cu in ore, 21.6 Mt go to milling and flotation and 6.8 Mt to leaching; the mill loses 3.6 Mt to tailings, the leach pads 1.8 Mt.
  • Scrap enters late. 4.4 Mt of scrap go to smelters and 1.0 Mt straight to fire refineries, a sixth of the feed at those two stages.
  • The chain is tightest in the middle. Smelting, converting and electrorefining each lose under 4 % to slag; the mill is where a fifth of the mined copper is lost.
  • Trade sits at two points. Concentrate crosses a border before smelting and cathode after refining; matte and anode mostly stay where they are made.

3 · Scrap versus primary input

The world runs on 0.23 t of scrap per tonne of primary feed, back at its 1995 level after a 2003 trough of 0.12.

The ratio fell through the early 2000s as primary smelting outran the scrap supply, then recovered. China's refineries feed 0.40 t of scrap per tonne of primary, Japan's 0.30, South Korea's 0.42 and the EU‑14's 0.60; the United States, at 0.05, has all but left secondary refining. Taiwan has no refinery-side feed in the model at all.

Read with care. In the reconciled solution China's scrap ratio (0.40) sits above its ICSG-based prior (0.31) and South Korea's (0.42) above its prior (0.34), Japan's (0.30) is close to its prior (0.28), while the EU‑14 posterior (0.60) sits below its prior (0.66); the world ratio (0.23) sits just above the prior (0.21). The solver adds scrap where the chain needs closing, and it adds it in China. The world level is a soft anchor; the regional split is a model outcome, not an observation.
  • The 2003 trough is real, not an artefact. Primary feed grew 43 % between 1995 and 2003 while the scrap feed fell by a quarter (2.1 → 1.6 Mt Cu).
  • China's ratio fell from 0.81 to 0.40 while its scrap volume rose 7.3× (0.5 → 3.5 Mt Cu): relative decline, absolute dominance.
  • The EU‑14 has held a scrap-heavy profile throughout (0.84 → 0.60), with Germany at 46 % scrap share in 2024, the highest of any large region.
  • The United States dropped from 0.23 to 0.05 after 2000, consistent with the closure of its secondary smelters; its scrap now goes to direct melt or export, both outside this boundary.
  • Japan rose steadily from 0.11 to 0.30. Its primary feed has been flat at 1.1–1.4 Mt Cu for thirty years while scrap feed tripled (128 → 385 kt); the ratio peaked at 0.36 in 2021, and the posterior follows the ICSG-based prior closely.
  • South Korea climbed fastest, from 0.17 to 0.42. Its scrap feed rose from 24 to 180 kt Cu while primary feed tripled (143 → 428 kt); the ratio climbed after a 2006 low of 0.08, peaked at 0.45 in 2019 and gives Korea the second-highest scrap share (30 %) among regions above 300 kt.
  • Taiwan has no secondary refining in the model. Its refinery-side feed is 0.5 kt Cu of primary and no scrap in 2024: Taiwan's copper use is fabrication from imported cathode and directly melted scrap, both outside the modelled boundary, so no ratio can be drawn for it.

4 · Supply concentration by chain step

Mining deconcentrated a little. Everything downstream concentrated into China.

In 1995 the United States led smelting, refining and final use. By 2024 China holds 51 % of world smelting, 44 % of refined cathode, 57 % of final use and 65 % of scrap feed, while the mining step became less concentrated. The EU‑14 kept a tenth of smelting and halved its share of use.

  • Upstream is diversifying. Mining HHI fell from 0.120 to 0.103. Chile's share of mined copper fell from 26 % to 21 % while Peru's rose from 4 % to 12 % and DR Congo's to 17 %, now second. Downstream, Chile keeps 5 % of smelting and 7 % of refined cathode, largely SX‑EW; Peru holds under 2 % of either.
  • The chokepoint moved from mine to smelter. Smelting HHI tripled (0.083 → 0.274) on China's rise from 9 % to 51 %, fed by imported concentrate (27 % → 84 % of intake).
  • The EU‑14 is a processor, not a consumer. It kept 10 % of smelting and 8 % of refining but fell from 22 % to 10 % of final use.
  • The United States exited the middle of the chain. From 16–23 % of smelting, refining and final use in 1995 to 2–6 % in 2024, while keeping 5 % of mining.
  • Secondary supply is the most concentrated step of all. Scrap feed HHI 0.43, with China taking 65 % of the world's refinery-bound scrap.

5 · Trade structure

Cross-border trade carries 21.7 Mt Cu a year, 2.9 times 1995, and concentrate has become the form in which copper travels.

Concentrate trade grew from 2.5 to 11.0 Mt Cu, from 29 % to 61 % of what is produced; cathode trade from 4.1 to 9.1 Mt. China imports 11.4 Mt, more than half of everything traded, where in 1995 the largest importer was Japan at 1.5 Mt. Chile and Peru send most of their concentrate to China, and the DR Congo's 2.0 Mt of cathode go there too.

  • The concentrate funnel. Chile → China (2.1 Mt Cu) and Peru → China (1.8 Mt) are the two largest legs in the system; China's smelters take 7.2 of the 11.0 Mt traded, and Chile sends 70 % of its concentrate exports there.
  • The cathode counter-flow. DR Congo → China (1.4 Mt), Chile → United States (0.6 Mt) and Chile → China (0.5 Mt) lead; Japan exports 0.7 Mt of cathode while importing 1.2 Mt of concentrate, the profile of a smelting hub.
  • Anode is a regional niche. 1.6 Mt in 2024: Zambia into China (0.24 Mt) and India (0.18), the DR Congo and Chile into China, Bulgaria into Germany.
  • Matte stays home. 155 kt Cu, under 1 % of what is smelted: the smelter and the converter sit on the same site.
  • The importers changed, the exporters less so. Japan took 1.5 Mt in 1995 and 1.2 Mt in 2024, the United States 0.7 and 0.9; China went from a marginal importer to 11.4 Mt. Chile was the largest exporter in both years (2.1 → 5.1 Mt), joined by Peru (2.8) and the DR Congo (2.5).

6 · End use categories

World copper end use grew 30 % since 2012 to 31.4 Mt Cu: the grid and the car led the growth (×1.74 each), building wiring stood still (×1.03) and plumbing shrank (×0.93).

IWCC assigns the copper content of semi-finished products to 16 end-use categories in six groups. Between 2012 and 2024 Power utility rose from 2.7 to 4.8 Mt Cu and Automotive electrical from 1.7 to 3.0 Mt, while Electrical power, the power wiring of buildings and still the largest single use at 5.8 Mt in 2024, held flat. These are IWCC data from outside the model: pySUTO does not reconcile them.

  • Building construction lost ground. Its five categories together fell from 31.5 % of world end use in 2012 to 25.9 % in 2023 and 24.7 % in 2024; Plumbing and Architecture also shrank in tonnage (×0.93 each vs 2012).
  • Infrastructure gained most. Power utility and telecommunications rose from 14.7 % of world end use in 2012 to 18.0 % in 2024; Power utility alone held 15.2 % in 2024, second only to Electrical power (18.4 %).
  • 2024 was the car's year. Automotive electrical jumped from 8.0 % to 9.6 % of world end use between 2023 and 2024, the largest one-year move of any category since 2012, and lifted Equipment — transport from 13.4 % to 14.6 %. World end use rose 3.6 % vs 2023, to 31,431 kt Cu.
  • Electronic equipment dipped. Its share of world end use fell from 5.7 % to 4.8 % between 2023 and 2024 and Electrical power's from 19.3 % to 18.4 %, the two largest losses of share that year.
